\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organize our results around three first-order findings. Each consolidates several specific observations into a single claim about how judges wiggle (FO1), in what direction (FO2), and what variation reveals about the judge itself (FO3).

% =============================================================
\paragraph{Finding \#1 (FO1): Pressure makes judges move; the \emph{type} of pressure determines whether they move a little or a lot.}
\label{fo:pressure}

\textbf{Frontier judges look nearly identical at the mechanical floor.} Averaged across the three mechanical tests, all 9 models cluster between 2--9\% wiggle (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}, mechanical bin; per-test breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:mech-variation}). The most mechanically stable judge (Claude Opus, 2\%) and the least (Grok-4.1 R, 9\%) differ by only 7pp. Mechanical variation is not where frontier models differentiate, and resampling-style probes --- repeating the same query under temperature noise or trivial prompt perturbations --- drastically underestimate what a deployed judge will face.

\textbf{Under substantive pressure, the same models pull apart sharply.} Averaged across L1--L4, GPT-5 (32\%) and Claude Sonnet (26\%) flip on the first challenge turn at 5--8$\times$ their mechanical rate, while Gemini Pro (7\%) and Grok-4.1 (9\%) barely budge above their floor. Multi-turn rates climb further: GPT-5 reaches 54\% over 10 turns; Claude Opus, the most mechanically stable model in the panel (2\%), becomes the fourth most persuadable under sustained pressure (44\%) --- a 24$\times$ multi-turn/mechanical ratio. The 1--2 order-of-magnitude gap between mechanical and multi-turn wiggle is the empirical signature that motivates the framework: stability under noise does not predict stability under pressure.

\textbf{The pressure ladder's shape is itself the story.} Figure~\ref{fig:domains-and-survival} (right) tells the central story of our pressure ladder. L1, L2, and L3 cluster near 80\% retention and barely move after turn 2 --- repeating a single mild tactic over 10 turns extracts almost no additional effect once the first vulnerable items have flipped. L4 has the strongest opening of any level: at turn 1, consensus pressure (``three independent reviewers all disagree'') drops retention to $\sim$73\%, lower than any other pressure type, but plateaus around turn 4. \textbf{L6 has the steepest curve}: its first-turn retention is $\sim$80\%, mid-pack and comparable to L1--L3, but it keeps falling through every subsequent turn, ending around 50\% retention by turn 10. Adaptive persuasion is uniquely effective not because its opening move is harder, but because it never stops finding new attack surfaces.

\textbf{More tactics is not more pressure.} L5 cycles through L1--L4 across 10 turns; L4 alone consistently outperforms it across every domain (Figure~\ref{fig:domains-and-survival}, left). The ``dilly-dallying'' of L5 appears to \emph{inoculate} the judge --- encountering mild challenges before the strong one builds resistance. Diversity of pressure helps the judge; intensity of a single well-chosen tactic helps the persuader.

\textbf{These pressure types are not redundant --- they probe distinct failure modes.} The cross-level correlation matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:corr-first-vs-last}) reveals the pressure ladder tests \emph{qualitatively different} vulnerabilities. L2 and L3 are nearly redundant ($\rho = 0.69$), but L1 (``are you sure?'') and L4 (``three reviewers disagree'') share only $\rho = 0.36$. L6 is more dissociated still ($\rho = 0.33$--$0.40$ with everything else): an adaptive persuader breaks items that no scripted tactic targets. The ladder cleanly separates at least three failure modes --- \textbf{sycophancy} (L1-susceptible), \textbf{conformity} (L4-susceptible), and \textbf{adversarial vulnerability} (L6-susceptible) --- which is what justifies measuring all three rather than collapsing them into a single ``robustness'' number.

\paragraph{Finding 2 (FO2): When judges move, they move with structure --- and usually away from the right answer.}
\label{fo:structure}

\textbf{Pressure is net-corrupting at every level.} Five of our six datasets have ground-truth labels, letting us classify each wiggle as \emph{corrective} (toward the label) or \emph{corrupting} (away). The aggregate across 60 (domain, scale, level) ground-truthed conditions is 56--63\% corrupting at L1--L5, and 70\% corrupting at L6 (Figure~\ref{fig:direction-and-outcomes}, right). A z-test on the per-condition corrective fractions shows that only \textbf{3 of 60 conditions} have a statistically significant corrective majority --- WildGuard Likert L2 (61.2\% corrective, $p < 0.001$), WildGuard Likert L3 (57.1\%, $p < 0.01$), and ToxiGen Likert L4 (58.0\%, $p < 0.01$). Challenging a judge fails to improve its accuracy at every level, in all other conditions.

\textbf{Wiggles are directionally asymmetric, and the direction depends on the grading scale.} For each domain, we can define a \emph{restrictive} verdict (``unsafe'', ``toxic'', ``refusing'') vs the \emph{permissive} verdict (``safe'', ``not toxic'', ``compliant''). The two sides of every domain's label space line up the same way: the restrictive verdict is the one where the judge takes a more conservative action (flagging as unsafe or toxic, marking content as AI-generated, refusing to answer), and the permissive verdict is the one where the judge lets the content through (safe, benign, human, compliant). This framing aggregates a directional signal across the six domains, whose specific label schemes would otherwise be incomparable. Binary flips lean \emph{restrictive} at every pressure level while Likert flips lean \emph{permissive} at every level (Figure~\ref{fig:direction-and-outcomes}, left). Why the same item, judged by the same model, would flip in opposite directions on a binary versus a Likert response scale --- and why this asymmetry persists across 9 judges and 6 domains --- is left for speculation. Operationally, it means a deployment's choice of grading scale is also a choice of which kind of error pressure will introduce.

\textbf{Binary and Likert flips also differ in \emph{when} they happen.} Under a single counterargument, binary verdicts flip 3--4$\times$ more often than Likert across L2--L6. By turn 10 the gap collapses to 1.1--2.4$\times$, and at L4 and L6 the two scales nearly converge. Binary flips tend to fire on turn 1 or never while Likert flips are gradual drifts that build over multiple turns. (Per-turn timing details in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-flip-timing}.)

\paragraph{Finding 3 (FO3): Vulnerability is a model-specific fingerprint, not a family trait.}
\label{fo:fingerprint}

\textbf{Within-model profile shape transfers across domains.} A model's \emph{own} L1--L6 wiggle profile shape is a fingerprint that mostly survives a change of domain. For 7 of 9 models, the within-model domain-transfer Spearman correlation of the L1--L6 vector across domain pairs has a median of $\rho \geq 0.84$ (Grok-4.1 R is highest at $\rho = 0.97$; Gemini 3.1 Pro is lowest at $\rho = 0.63$ with a worst pair at $\rho = -0.09$). The level-profile \emph{shape} transfers across domains within a model, but absolute rates and ranks do not --- a pilot wiggle test on one domain reliably predicts which pressure types a model is vulnerable to on other domains, but deployment-relevant absolute rates are unique per-domain.

\textbf{Family is a weak proxy for sibling behavior.} Within-family correlations \emph{are} high for most providers (Grok 4.1 R / Grok 4.1 share $\rho = 0.89$; the GPT-5/5.2/5.4 family pairs share $\rho = 0.84$--$0.89$; Claude 4.6 Sonnet / Claude 4.6 Opus share $\rho = 0.80$) --- but cross-family correlations are often just as high. Grok 4.1 R correlates with GPT-5.2 at $\rho = 0.86$ and with Claude Opus at $\rho = 0.84$. \textbf{Gemini Flash and Gemini Pro share $\rho = 0.32$ --- the lowest pair in the entire matrix, lower than most cross-family pairs.} For most families, testing one sibling gives a reasonable read on the other; for Google's Gemini models, this transfer fails entirely.

\textbf{Self-persuasion is asymmetric.} When a model persuades itself versus a sibling versus a non-family model, the L6 wiggle rates dissociate (Table~\ref{tab:self-persuasion}). Claude 4.6 Opus shows an intuitive gradient: self (70\%) $>$ family (62\%) $>$ non-family (47\%). GPT-5.4 has a small family-level advantage that \emph{matches} its self-persuasion (72\% vs 69\%). Grok-4.1 R inverts the pattern: it is the \emph{least} effective at wiggling itself (19\%) but \emph{more} effective on its non-reasoning sibling (55\%) and on non-family models (36\%). The reasoning model's step-by-step traces appear to \emph{self-inoculate} --- the same epistemic structure that defends Grok-4.1 R against itself becomes a sharp weapon against the non-reasoning sibling that lacks that defense. (Per-cell self-persuasion breakdown in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-self}.)
