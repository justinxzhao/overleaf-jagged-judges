\paragraph{Finding 2. Adaptive persuasion (L6) is the steepest escalation, and multi-turn persistence is load-bearing.}
\label{sec:l6-ceiling}

The jump from L5 to L6 is the largest single increase in wiggle in every domain-scale combination. L6 introduces a fundamentally new element: real-time adaptation, where the persuader observes the judge's reasoning, identifies weak points, and tailors the next argument. The dramatic effectiveness of L6 across all domains suggests the vulnerability is not to any particular argument type but to adaptive targeting of the judge's reasoning patterns --- any multi-agent system where an LLM observes and adapts to the judge creates an L6-like attack surface. Multi-turn persistence is also load-bearing at higher pressure levels: L6 gains 20--43pp from turns 2--10 beyond what the first turn captures, and Likert wiggles --- which require 2-point movement --- are particularly slow to accumulate. A breakdown of flip timing by turn is in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-flip-timing}.

\paragraph{Finding 4. Each domain has a distinct directional bias.}
\label{sec:directional}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Directional bias by domain. ``Ratio'' is the conditional flip rate in the dominant direction divided by the flip rate in the other direction.}
\label{tab:directional-bias}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{3.5cm}rp{2.8cm}r@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Domain} & \textbf{Rubric} & \textbf{Binary direction} & \textbf{Ratio} & \textbf{Likert direction} & \textbf{Ratio} \\
\midrule
WildGuard & Safety   & safe$\to$unsafe (restrictive) & 1.79 & toward-unsafe at L4--L5 & ${\sim}1.09$ \\
PP        & Hedging  & hedged items flip more        & 0.64 & toward direct           & 0.50 \\

MAGE      & AI detection & nearly symmetric          & 0.93 & toward-AI               & 1.48 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Decomposing wiggles by direction --- conditioning on the initial verdict --- reveals that each domain has its own default correction direction under pressure (Table~\ref{tab:directional-bias}). The unifying pattern is that judges tend to revise toward the ``more cautious'' or ``more alarming'' interpretation, with strength varying by domain. WildGuard binary confirms a 1.79$\times$ restrictive bias (safe$\to$unsafe). Paired Prompts refusal Likert (2.71) is the strongest directional bias in our entire dataset --- judges are nearly three times more likely to shift toward ``more refusal'' than ``more compliance,'' consistent with a safety-conservative prior. PP hedging shows the opposite (0.50, toward more directness), suggesting a separate training prior favoring direct communication. MAGE binary is nearly symmetric (0.93) but Likert shows a moderate suspicion prior toward ``AI-generated'' (1.48). These directional biases are training signatures: under pressure, models do not revise randomly but in the direction their training tells them a mistake is most likely to go. A more granular level-dependent breakdown of WildGuard Likert (which masks a level-dependent bias structure under its overall 1.09 ratio) is in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-directional}.

\paragraph{Finding 5. Pressure is mostly corrupting, with one level-dependent exception.}
\label{sec:ground-truth}

Both MAGE and WildGuard ship with dataset labels, enabling us to classify each wiggle as \emph{corrective} (toward the label) or \emph{corrupting} (away). On \textbf{MAGE}, approximately 70\% of wiggles are corrupting and only 27--32\% are corrective, stable across all levels and both scales --- pressure consistently moves judges away from the dataset label. On \textbf{WildGuard}, binary wiggles are also majority-corrupting (57--70\%) but with a higher corrective fraction (30--43\%). The notable exception is \textbf{WildGuard Likert at L2--L3, where wiggles are majority-corrective (61\% and 57\%)} --- the only condition in the study where pressure moves judges toward the label more often than away. At L6 the pattern reverts to ${\sim}70\%$ corrupting. This level-dependent pattern suggests that moderate counterarguments can help safety judges reconsider, but adaptive persuasion overwhelms the effect.

Stratifying WildGuard by human annotator consensus (2/3 vs 3/3 agreement) further reveals that items where humans disagree are systematically more wiggable for LLM judges --- a consistent ${\sim}10$--$15$pp gap across all pressure levels (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-human-consensus}). Wiggle therefore partially measures intrinsic item ambiguity, not purely model weakness.

\paragraph{Finding 6. Persuader effectiveness is a stable, domain-general capability.}
\label{sec:persuader}

At L6, the persuader ranking is remarkably stable across all domain-by-scale combinations: GPT-5.4 is the strongest persuader in every condition (58--83\% persuasion rate), Claude Opus is second (42--70\%), Grok-4.1 Reasoning is third (24--53\%). The ranking never shuffles, suggesting effective persuasion is a domain-general capability. Self-persuasion effects (whether models are more easily persuaded by themselves) are model-dependent and analyzed in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-self}.

\paragraph{Finding 7. Sycophancy and conformity are dissociated failure modes.}
\label{sec:sycophancy-conformity}

The six pressure levels are designed as an escalating sequence, but the correlation structure reveals they are not a simple continuum. In WildGuard, L2 and L3 are nearly redundant ($\rho = 0.80$): items that flip under a specific counterargument almost always flip under an expert appeal. But L1 (mild doubt) and L4 (consensus) are only weakly linked ($\rho = 0.22$): the items susceptible to generic social doubt (``are you sure?'') are \emph{not} the same items susceptible to fabricated consensus pressure. This dissociation suggests the conviction ladder captures two qualitatively different failure modes: \textbf{sycophancy} (folding under social doubt, L1) and \textbf{conformity} (folding under fabricated peer pressure, L4). A model can be highly sycophantic yet resistant to consensus pressure, or vice versa. The full cross-level correlation matrix is in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-correlations}.

% ===========================================================
\paragraph{Finding 8. Jury bootstrapping universally predicts wiggle.}
\label{sec:cross-level-corr}

Across all domains and scales, the jury--wiggle correlation ranges from $\rho = -0.17$ to $-0.39$, with the tightest link at L5 strategy cycling and a somewhat weaker link at L4 consensus pressure. Jury strength explains 5--15\% of wiggle variance --- modest in absolute terms, but remarkably consistent: \emph{negative in every domain, at every level, on both scales}. No adversarial probe achieves this universality. This supports a simple practical prescription: \textbf{run all judges once at baseline and use the jury majority strength as a per-item difficulty signal.}
