\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Epistemic Behavior Is Jagged}
\label{sec:discussion-jagged}

A loose observation about LLMs in general is that they are \emph{jagged}: capable models are sharply good at some things and shockingly bad at others --- solving olympiad-level math but miscounting letters in a word, drafting nuanced essays but failing arithmetic the average middle-schooler can do. The term has not received a precise operational definition, partly because the phenomenon resists exact reproduction across changes of prompt, sampling temperature, and task framing.

This paper is not a study of capability; it is a study of \emph{epistemic behavior} --- whether judge verdicts are stable under silence, single-turn challenge, and multi-turn pressure. But the jaggedness applies just as cleanly. We propose a working definition tailored to our setting: \textbf{a judge's jaggedness is the standard deviation of its mean wiggle rate across the (domain, scale) cells we tested.} The definition is loose by necessity --- non-determinism, prompt sensitivity, and the choice of test domains all influence the value --- but within our 12-cell grid it cleanly separates judges with stable epistemic profiles from judges whose profiles vary sharply across tasks.

The findings in \S\ref{sec:results} unpack four concrete forms of jaggedness:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No single judge is universally best.} Mean-retention values across the 12 (domain, scale) cells span 0.085 (GPT-5 on MAGE binary) to 0.860 (Gemini 3.1 Pro on MAGE binary) --- a 10$\times$ within-task gap. Within-model spans are also large (Claude Sonnet 0.171 to 0.904; GPT-5 0.033 to 0.714), and family is a weak proxy: Gemini Flash$\leftrightarrow$Pro share $\rho = 0.32$, lower than most cross-family pairs (Table~\ref{tab:aurc-selected}).
\item \textbf{Mild doubt, fabricated consensus, and adaptive persuasion catch different items.} Cross-level Spearman correlations between L1, L4, and L6 sit at $\rho = 0.33$--$0.40$ (Finding~7) --- these are loosely related failure modes, not a single underlying axis. A judge can be sycophantic without being conformity-prone, conformity-prone without being adversarially fragile, or vice versa.
\item \textbf{The scale of the verdict determines which direction wiggle takes.} Binary flips lean restrictive at every L1--L6 (15--30\%); Likert flips lean permissive at every L1--L6 (13--35\%). The same items, same judges, same pressure produce opposite directional biases under the two scales (Finding~5). The \emph{absolute} and \emph{corrective} directions of wiggle are independent dimensions and can disagree on the same items.
\item \textbf{Within-model profile shape transfers across domains; ranks and rates do not.} A model's L1--L6 profile shape is a fingerprint (median $\rho \geq 0.84$ within-model across domains, Finding~9), but absolute rates and rank orderings reshuffle from one (domain, scale) cell to another (Findings~10 and~3 of \S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

Together, these refute the simplest framings of LLM-judge instability --- \emph{the model is sycophantic}, \emph{the model is overconfident}, \emph{the model is just noisy} --- each true of part of the data and false of the rest. The practical orientation is not a single calibration recipe but a refusal of one-dimensional summaries: epistemic behavior, like LLM capability more broadly, is jagged, and a judge cannot be summarized by a scalar.

\subsection{Jury Majority Strength at Baseline Is a Simple Reliability Screen for Golden Sets}
\label{sec:discussion-jury}

If wiggle is jagged, some items in any golden set are inherently more epistemically uncertain than others, even within a single (domain, scale) cell. The practical question that follows is whether one can \emph{predict which items wiggle} using a smaller test, since running the full Wiggle Framework battery --- six pressure levels, ten challenge turns, three persuaders, and a full jury panel --- requires substantial extra compute beyond the standard golden-set validation. A single one-shot proxy, run once, that flags items the framework would later show to be unstable would let practitioners tighten their golden sets without paying the full cost of the framework.

We test three candidate predictors of per-item wiggle rate: \emph{baseline jury majority strength} (the size of the L0 majority across the 9 judges, with no pressure applied), \emph{repeat consistency} (per-item temperature-zero repeat agreement), and \emph{position invariance} (the rate at which the verdict survives reordering of the two opposing arguments). Jury majority strength is the strongest predictor at every level (Table~\ref{tab:predictor-comparison}). The relationship is universal: all 84 of 84 (domain $\times$ scale $\times$ level) cells show negative Spearman $\rho$ between jury majority strength and per-item wiggle rate, with median $|\rho| = 0.58$ and a range from $-0.01$ to $-0.86$ (per-cell heatmap in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-jury-heatmap}). Jury wins by ${\sim}0.17$ $\rho$ over the next-best predictor (repeat consistency, mean $|\rho| = 0.42$) at the overall level and at every individual pressure level, and the gap between unanimous and split juries is substantial: PP Hedging binary shows the largest mean gap (41pp), followed by PP Refusal binary and ToxiGen binary (36pp each), with all 14 (domain, scale) conditions averaging at least 18pp.

Repeat and invariance are weaker but real signals --- they probe overlapping facets of the same underlying fragility. For practitioners who cannot easily run a 9-judge ensemble, mechanical stability is a defensible alternative: at mean $|\rho| = 0.42$ (repeat) and $|\rho| = 0.37$ (invariance), it captures item-level fragility well enough to be useful, and at lower cost than a 9-judge baseline. The two methods are complementary in deployment: jury majority strength when it is feasible, mechanical stability when it is not.

The protocol is direct: run all candidate judges once at L0 baseline on your golden set; flag items where jury majority strength is low; either remove those items (tightening the golden set to robust labels) or downweight them in accuracy calculations. Cost: 9 API calls per item, no adversarial infrastructure. The recommendation is anchored on Finding~6 --- since pressure is net-corrupting, the right move when the jury splits is to \emph{flag} the item, not to \emph{interrogate} it through debate or self-critique loops.

\paragraph{A methodological caution: rubric-splitting matters.} An earlier version of this analysis treated Paired Prompts as a single combined domain and appeared to show the jury relationship reversing on PP Likert ($\rho$ values trending positive). When we separated PP into its hedging and refusal sub-rubrics --- which use different rubrics and therefore different jury compositions --- the apparent reversal disappeared and both sub-rubrics showed strong negative $\rho$ everywhere. The combined-domain artifact arose because hedging and refusal juries vote on different questions; aggregating their verdicts diluted the jury-majority-strength metric to noise. Multi-rubric domains must be analyzed rubric-by-rubric for jury-based screening to work correctly.

\subsection{Epistemic Fragility Matters Beyond the Single-Shot Verdict}
\label{sec:discussion-pressure}

A reasonable critique of this paper would ask: \emph{why does any of this matter?} In production, LLM judges are almost never deployed in an epistemic sense. They are asked to emit a single verdict, much like a classical classifier --- ``safe'' or ``unsafe'', ``toxic'' or ``not\_toxic'', a 1--5 score. They are not asked to defend, debate, or reconsider. If that is how judges are actually used, why does it matter how they behave under sustained challenge?

The answer returns to the framing in \S\ref{sec:introduction}. LLM judges occupy a unique middle ground between conventional classifiers and human raters: they emit a discrete verdict like a classifier, but they can also explain it, defend it, and engage in conversation about it. Production deployments typically strip away that conversational tail and consume only the verdict --- but the verdict is shaped by the same underlying machinery that produces the explanations, justifications, and rated confidences. Our experiments measure how much weight that machinery deserves, and the answer is sobering: \textbf{the epistemic structure surrounding a verdict --- the defense, the explanation, the rated confidence --- looks substantial on the surface but collapses under sustained pressure across the great majority of our domains.} Even though models do respond to epistemic challenge, the wiggles they produce are net-corrupting more than corrective at every level we tested (Finding~6).

The implication is interpretive rather than algorithmic. Aligning a judge to a golden set and reporting accuracy is necessary but not sufficient: the same judge that scores well in single-shot mode may carry beliefs that are easily eroded by a single counter-argument or, worse, by an adaptive persuader. The overconfidence literature \citep{wei2024simpleqa,huang2025irt} has shown that judges produce confident probability estimates that don't reflect their stability under resampling; our work confirms that result and extends it to the multi-turn setting, where the gap between surface conviction and underlying robustness is even larger than the resampling gap.

\paragraph{Where epistemic robustness becomes load-bearing.} The cases where production deployment \emph{does} enter epistemic territory are the cases where this matters most. Multi-agent judging architectures, debate-based scalable oversight \citep{khan2024debating,browncohen2024debate}, reward refinement via self-critique, and any pipeline that surfaces inter-judge disagreement to a focal judge all subject the participating models to L4--L6 pressure in production form. Our data shows substantial inter-model variation in robustness under such pressure: Grok-4.1 R and Gemini 3.1 Pro maintain mean retention $\geq 0.83$ across the 12 cells we tested, while GPT-5 sits at 0.49 and Claude Opus at 0.58 (Table~\ref{tab:aurc-selected}). For domains like safety classification or political-content evaluation, where consensus is hard and discourse is genuinely required, the choice of judge meaningfully shapes whether deliberation converges on a more reliable answer or a less reliable one.

Two concrete recommendations follow. First, \textbf{do not use persuasion or debate to refine judge verdicts in hill-climbing settings.} The corrective window we found exists only on WildGuard Likert L2--L3 and does not generalize even to other safety domains; aggregate corruption ratios are 1.3--2.3:1 at every level. Second, \textbf{test any judge intended for an agentic loop against an L6-style adaptive persuader before trusting it}: every frontier judge in our panel breaks at 62--91\% under L6, including its own majority-vote ensemble. The L6 prompt template (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}) is sufficient to reproduce the attack against any judge of interest.

The broader takeaway: as LLM judges become load-bearing infrastructure for capability measurement, post-training reward refinement, and the agentic loops that train and evaluate frontier models, the structural shape of their inconsistency --- not just its overall magnitude --- should inform how we design the loops that consume their signal.
