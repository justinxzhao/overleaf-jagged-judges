\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The findings in \S\ref{sec:results} translate into three practical takeaways, each tied directly to one of the three claims we made in the abstract.

\subsection{Epistemic Stability Is Jagged: Resist Simple Explanations of Inconsistency}
\label{sec:discussion-jagged}

The most common framings of LLM-judge instability --- \emph{the model is sycophantic}, \emph{the model is overconfident}, \emph{the model is just noisy} --- are each true of part of the data and false of the rest. Mild doubt, fabricated consensus, and adaptive persuasion catch different items at low cross-correlation (Finding~\ref{sec:finding-dissociation}, $\rho = 0.33$--$0.40$ between L1, L4, and L6). Mechanical re-prompting and adversarial pressure measure quantities that differ by an order of magnitude (Finding~\ref{sec:finding-mechanical-gap}). On safety Likert scales, flips are predominantly \emph{permissive} even when pressure is net-corrupting (Findings~\ref{sec:finding-permissive} and~\ref{sec:finding-corrupting}) --- the \emph{absolute} and \emph{corrective} directions of wiggle are independent dimensions and can disagree on the same items. And a model's level-profile \emph{shape} transfers across domains while its absolute rates and ranks do not (Findings~\ref{sec:finding-family} and~\ref{sec:finding-within-model}).

Concretely, no single judge is universally best. AURC values across the 12 (domain, scale) cells span 0.085 (GPT-5 on MAGE binary) to 0.860 (Gemini 3.1 Pro on MAGE binary) --- a 10$\times$ within-task gap. Within-model spans are also large (Claude Sonnet 0.171 to 0.904; GPT-5 0.033 to 0.714), and family is a weak proxy: Gemini Flash$\leftrightarrow$Pro share $\rho = 0.32$, lower than most cross-family pairs.

The practical orientation is not a single calibration recipe but a refusal of one-dimensional summaries: do not assume one frontier model dominates as a judge across tasks; report a degradation curve rather than a scalar; and treat per-domain AURC as a deployment input rather than an inference from family or aggregate accuracy.

\subsection{Jury Disagreement at Baseline Is a Cheap Reliability Screen for Golden Sets}
\label{sec:discussion-jury}

The single most universal predictor of wiggle in our data is \emph{baseline jury disagreement} --- the spread of L0 verdicts across the 9 judges, with no pressure applied. Items where the jury is unanimous are 10--22pp less wiggable than items where the jury splits, and this gap holds across all 12 (domain, scale) conditions at every pressure level L1--L6 (Spearman $\rho = -0.17$ to $-0.39$, negative everywhere). No adversarial probe achieves this universality.

The protocol is direct: run all candidate judges once at L0 baseline on your golden set; flag items where the jury splits; either remove those items (tightening the golden set to robust labels) or downweight them in accuracy calculations. Cost: 9 API calls per item, no adversarial infrastructure. The recommendation is anchored on Finding~\ref{sec:finding-corrupting} --- since pressure is net-corrupting, the right move when the jury disagrees is to \emph{flag} the item, not to \emph{interrogate} it through debate or self-critique loops.

\subsection{Epistemic Pressure Is Net-Corrupting in Hill-Climbing and Reward Loops}
\label{sec:discussion-pressure}

LLM-judge verdicts are increasingly used as a training signal. Two proposals to \emph{improve} that signal are actively discussed: more inference-time compute per call, and adversarial procedures (persuasion, debate, self-critique) that expose the judge to challenge. Our data is direct evidence against the second proposal in its asymmetric forms. Across 60 ground-truthed conditions, only 3 are statistically corrective (Finding~\ref{sec:finding-corrupting}); aggregate corruption ratios are 1.3--2.3:1 at every level. At L6, $\sim 70\%$ of flips are corrupting and an 8-judge majority defense collapses by 25--55pp. Increasing the \emph{sophistication} of the persuader does the opposite of what a ``more persuasive $\to$ more truthful'' hypothesis would predict.

We separate this from the symmetric two-sided debate result of \citet{radhakrishnan2023debating}: their setup pits opposing debaters against each other on QuALITY, while our pressure ladder applies single-sided pressure against the judge's current verdict. Our positional-invariance test (\S\ref{sec:framework}) is the closest analog to a symmetric setup but measures consistency rather than accuracy gain --- so we cannot speak directly to the symmetric claim. We speak to the asymmetric case, which is the form most often deployed in agent loops, self-critique, and reward refinement, and where the data is unambiguous.

Two recommendations follow. First, \textbf{do not use persuasion or debate to refine judge verdicts in hill-climbing settings.} The corrective window we found exists only on WildGuard Likert L2--L3 and does not generalize even to other safety domains. Second, \textbf{test any judge used as a reward signal against an L6-style adaptive persuader} before trusting it: every frontier judge in our panel breaks at 62--91\% under L6, including its own majority-vote ensemble. The L6 prompt template (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}) is sufficient to reproduce the attack against any judge of interest.

This same logic argues against \textbf{multi-agent judging architectures}, where multiple LLM agents debate, critique, or vote on a verdict in real time. Such systems are L4--L6 pressure in production form, and they implicitly assume that the participating judges hold robust enough priors for deliberation to converge on a more accurate answer. Our jaggedness data (Findings~\ref{sec:finding-family} and~\ref{sec:finding-within-model}, Table~\ref{tab:aurc-selected}) shows the opposite: none of the frontier judges we tested has stable opinions across the 14 tasks we ran. Verdicts are heavily context-window-sensitive --- exactly the failure mode that multi-agent deliberation amplifies. Multi-agent judging may add explanatory color around a verdict, but our evidence does not support it as a path to a more reliable final answer.

\subsection{Relation to Calibration Practice and the Scope of This Work}
\label{sec:discussion-scope}

A natural anchor for the present work is the classical practice of classifier calibration. The closest analog --- using the token-level probability of the verdict as a confidence signal --- is one of several methods practitioners currently reach for, alongside re-prompt resampling and sycophancy probing. Each is operationally constrained: logits are not exposed by every frontier provider; reasoning models emit token sequences before the verdict that complicate any ``verdict-token'' probability lookup; logits depend on the tokenizer, so swapping models requires re-mapping token IDs; and a verdict label like \emph{unsafe} may have multiple valid tokenizations whose probabilities must be summed to recover a single confidence score. These are real engineering frictions, but they are not the reason we do not propose token-probability calibration as our method.

The reason is that token probability, re-prompt stability, and sycophancy probing are all measuring overlapping forms of the same underlying property --- \emph{wiggle} --- and the field has not yet placed them under a single experimental frame. Our framework's contribution is that unification, not a numerical confidence score. Single-shot accuracy on golden sets remains the right first-line validation method for most teams deploying LLM judges; \textbf{we do not argue that every team should add stability checks as a blocker for deployment.} We argue that \emph{when} a team does want to interrogate confidence --- for golden-set curation, for understanding how a judge will behave in pipelines that introduce conversational pressure, or for choosing between candidate models --- the methods that already exist for doing so can finally be compared on common ground.
