\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The findings in \S\ref{sec:results} translate into three practical takeaways, each tied directly to one of the three claims we made in the abstract.

\subsection{Epistemic Stability Is Jagged: Resist Simple Explanations of Inconsistency}
\label{sec:discussion-jagged}

The most common framings of LLM-judge instability --- \emph{the model is sycophantic}, \emph{the model is overconfident}, \emph{the model is just noisy} --- are each true of part of the data and false of the rest. Mild doubt, fabricated consensus, and adaptive persuasion catch different items at low cross-correlation (Finding~7, $\rho = 0.33$--$0.40$ between L1, L4, and L6). Mechanical re-prompting and adversarial pressure measure quantities that differ by an order of magnitude (Finding~1). On safety Likert scales, flips are predominantly \emph{permissive} even when pressure is net-corrupting (Findings~5 and~6) --- the \emph{absolute} and \emph{corrective} directions of wiggle are independent dimensions and can disagree on the same items. And a model's level-profile \emph{shape} transfers across domains while its absolute rates and ranks do not (Findings~9 and~10).

Concretely, no single judge is universally best. Mean-retention values across the 12 (domain, scale) cells span 0.085 (GPT-5 on MAGE binary) to 0.860 (Gemini 3.1 Pro on MAGE binary) --- a 10$\times$ within-task gap. Within-model spans are also large (Claude Sonnet 0.171 to 0.904; GPT-5 0.033 to 0.714), and family is a weak proxy: Gemini Flash$\leftrightarrow$Pro share $\rho = 0.32$, lower than most cross-family pairs.

The practical orientation is not a single calibration recipe but a refusal of one-dimensional summaries: do not assume one frontier model dominates as a judge across tasks.

\subsection{Jury Disagreement at Baseline Is a Simple Reliability Screen for Golden Sets}
\label{sec:discussion-jury}

The strongest predictor of wiggle in our data is \emph{baseline jury disagreement} --- the spread of L0 verdicts across the 9 judges, with no pressure applied. The relationship is universal: all 84 of 84 (domain $\times$ scale $\times$ level) cells show negative Spearman $\rho$, with median $|\rho| = 0.58$ and a range from $-0.01$ to $-0.86$ (Figure~\ref{fig:jury-rho-heatmap}). The gap between unanimous and split juries is substantial: PP Hedging binary shows the largest mean gap (41pp), followed by PP Refusal binary and ToxiGen binary (36pp each), with all 14 (domain, scale) conditions averaging at least 18pp.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering

  \caption{Spearman $\rho$ between baseline jury disagreement and per-item wiggle rate, for each (domain, scale, level) cell. All 84 cells are negative: items where the jury splits at L0 are more wiggable under pressure, without exception.}
  \label{fig:jury-rho-heatmap}
\end{figure}

Jury disagreement is the strongest predictor by a clear margin (Figure~\ref{fig:predictor-comparison}). Temperature-zero repeat consistency reaches mean $|\rho| = 0.415$ (significant in 63 of 72 cells); position-invariance reaches $|\rho| = 0.365$ (significant in 54 of 72 cells); jury reaches $|\rho| = 0.590$ (significant in 83 of 84 cells, 99\%). Jury wins by 0.175 $\rho$ over the next-best predictor and is statistically significant nearly everywhere, but jury, repeat-stability, and position-invariance all measure overlapping facets of the same underlying fragility. For practitioners who cannot easily run a 9-judge ensemble, mechanical stability is a defensible alternative.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\linewidth]{predictor_comparison.png}
  \caption{Mean $|\rho|$ between each candidate predictor and per-item wiggle rate. Baseline jury disagreement is the strongest predictor, ahead of temperature-zero repeat consistency and position invariance, and is statistically significant in 99\% of cells.}
  \label{fig:predictor-comparison}
\end{figure}

The protocol is direct: run all candidate judges once at L0 baseline on your golden set; flag items where the jury splits; either remove those items (tightening the golden set to robust labels) or downweight them in accuracy calculations. Cost: 9 API calls per item, no adversarial infrastructure. The recommendation is anchored on Finding~6 --- since pressure is net-corrupting, the right move when the jury disagrees is to \emph{flag} the item, not to \emph{interrogate} it through debate or self-critique loops.

\paragraph{A methodological caution: rubric-splitting matters.} An earlier version of this analysis treated Paired Prompts as a single combined domain and appeared to show the jury relationship reversing on PP Likert ($\rho$ values trending positive). When we separated PP into its hedging and refusal sub-rubrics --- which use different rubrics and therefore different jury compositions --- the apparent reversal disappeared and both sub-rubrics showed strong negative $\rho$ everywhere. The combined-domain artifact arose because hedging and refusal juries vote on different questions; aggregating their verdicts diluted the jury-strength metric to noise. Multi-rubric domains must be analyzed rubric-by-rubric for jury-based screening to work correctly.

\subsection{Epistemic Pressure Is Net-Corrupting in Hill-Climbing and Reward Loops}
\label{sec:discussion-pressure}

LLM-judge verdicts are increasingly used as a training signal. Two proposals to \emph{improve} that signal are actively discussed: more inference-time compute per call, and adversarial procedures (persuasion, debate, self-critique) that expose the judge to challenge. Our data is direct evidence against the second proposal in its asymmetric forms. Across 60 ground-truthed conditions, only 3 are statistically corrective (Finding~6); aggregate corruption ratios are 1.3--2.3:1 at every level. At L6, $\sim 70\%$ of flips are corrupting and an 8-judge majority defense collapses by 25--55pp. Increasing the \emph{sophistication} of the persuader does the opposite of what a ``more persuasive $\to$ more truthful'' hypothesis would predict.

Two recommendations follow. First, \textbf{do not use persuasion or debate to refine judge verdicts in hill-climbing settings.} The corrective window we found exists only on WildGuard Likert L2--L3 and does not generalize even to other safety domains. Second, \textbf{test any judge used as a reward signal against an L6-style adaptive persuader} before trusting it: every frontier judge in our panel breaks at 62--91\% under L6, including its own majority-vote ensemble. The L6 prompt template (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}) is sufficient to reproduce the attack against any judge of interest.

This same logic argues against \textbf{multi-agent judging architectures}, where multiple LLM agents debate, critique, or vote on a verdict in real time. Such systems are L4--L6 pressure in production form, and they implicitly assume that the participating judges hold robust enough priors for deliberation to converge on a more accurate answer. Our jaggedness data (Findings~9 and~10, Table~\ref{tab:aurc-selected}) shows the opposite: none of the frontier judges we tested has stable opinions across the 14 tasks we ran. Verdicts are heavily context-window-sensitive --- exactly the failure mode that multi-agent deliberation amplifies. Multi-agent judging may add explanatory color around a verdict, but our evidence does not support it as a path to a more reliable final answer.
