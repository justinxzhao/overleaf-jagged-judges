\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Conversational Context Corrupts Classification}

Wiggle reveals a fundamental tension in the architecture of LLM judges. These models are trained to be helpful conversational partners --- to update beliefs based on new information, consider alternative perspectives, defer to expertise. These are desirable properties for a chatbot but pathological for a classifier. When we use an LLM as a judge, we ask it to act as a fixed-point function invariant to conversational framing, while its training optimizes for contextual responsiveness. Wiggle is the direct measurement of that tension. The fact that even L1 (``Are you sure?'') changes 24--50\% of binary verdicts means conversational context --- including any preceding discussion, implicit social framing, or prompt structure resembling a challenge --- is a latent variable affecting the judgment itself. This matters most for agentic deployments, where judges encounter conversational pressure naturally from their own reasoning loop or other agents in the system; we map our L1--L6 ladder onto agentic scenarios in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-agentic}.

\subsection{Jaggedness, Quantified}

The notion of LLMs as ``jagged'' intelligence --- displaying superhuman performance on some tasks while failing on others that humans find trivial --- has become commonplace in discussions of frontier capabilities. Rigorous quantification has been limited, and arguably resists clean measurement: jaggedness is, almost by definition, the property that makes any single benchmark insufficient. The term is often invoked to evoke high variance in model behavior in a way that emphasizes why patterns are hard to extract.

Our experiments offer one concrete operationalization in the LLM-as-judge setting. We define jaggedness as the population variance of a judge's mean wiggle rate across (domain, scale) combinations (Eq.~\ref{eq:jaggedness}). The data (Table~\ref{tab:aurc-selected}) suggest jaggedness is a separate dimension of judge quality from overall robustness. Grok-4.1 has very low jaggedness (0.006) despite mid-tier overall robustness (Overall AURC 0.689): it is uniformly mid-tier across the grid. Gemini Pro shows similar near-uniformity at much higher overall robustness (0.805, jaggedness 0.009). At the other extreme, GPT-5 has both the lowest overall robustness (0.332) and the highest jaggedness (0.070), and GPT-5.4 has moderate overall robustness (0.584) with high jaggedness (0.043) --- making its behavior particularly hard to predict from any single benchmark.

We do not claim our metric captures all senses of ``jagged intelligence.'' What we offer is one quantitative footing for the term, restricted to a specific epistemic context (judging tasks) and a specific behavioral signal (wiggle under pressure). The hope is that more concrete measurements --- across more domains, more behavioral dimensions, and more model classes --- can give the term load-bearing meaning beyond an informal observation.

\subsection{Model Selection: Robustness Is Not Transferable}

The reshuffling of robustness rankings across domains (Finding 3) means judge selection must be empirical and domain-specific. The most robust judge-domain combination (Gemini Flash on WildGuard binary, AURC 0.877) still flips 73\% of its verdicts at L6; the least robust (GPT-5 on MAGE Likert, AURC 0.034) changes 95--100\%. Intra-family differences are striking: Claude Sonnet vs Opus differ by AURC 0.448 vs 0.602 on WildGuard binary, indicating conviction robustness is sensitive to alignment tuning, not just base architecture. Wiggle rate is a complementary signal to accuracy: a 95\%-accurate judge with 40\% L4 wiggle is less operationally reliable than a 90\%-accurate judge with 10\% L4 wiggle. AURC reports must be per-domain, since rankings reshuffle. Combining a high-conviction judge with a more responsive one tends to improve joint reliability over averaging more judges of the same type --- ensemble gains come from \emph{model-level} diversity.

\subsection{The Bias-Variance Tradeoff in Judge Design}

A judge that is nearly immovable may also be the least responsive to legitimate new information. For absolute calibration (estimating the prevalence of unsafe content), responsiveness to good arguments is a feature. For comparative evaluation (ranking two models), what matters is that the judge's bias is \emph{consistent} across the models being compared. Multi-turn persistence is therefore not straightforwardly a virtue: practitioners should choose the right level of stubbornness for their deployment context. The Wiggle Framework makes this tradeoff explicit by providing the full degradation curve rather than a single number.
