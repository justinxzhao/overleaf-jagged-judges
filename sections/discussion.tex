\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Epistemic Behavior Is Jagged}
\label{sec:discussion-jagged}

LLMs are widely observed to be \emph{jagged}: capable models are sharply good at some tasks and shockingly bad at others. The term has not received a precise operational definition, partly because the phenomenon resists exact reproduction across changes of prompt, sampling, and framing. This paper studies \emph{epistemic behavior} rather than capability, but the jaggedness applies. We define \textbf{a judge's jaggedness as the standard deviation of its mean wiggle rate across the (domain, scale) cells we tested} (Figure~\ref{fig:wiggliness-jaggedness}) --- loose by necessity, but cleanly separating judges with stable epistemic profiles from judges whose profiles vary sharply across tasks.

Four concrete forms of jaggedness emerge from \S\ref{sec:results}: (i) no single judge is universally best --- mean retention spans 0.085 to 0.860 across the 12 cells (Table~\ref{tab:aurc-selected}), within-model spans are large (Claude Sonnet 0.171 to 0.904), and family is a weak proxy (Gemini Flash$\leftrightarrow$Pro $\rho = 0.32$); (ii) L1, L4, and L6 catch different items (cross-level $\rho = 0.33$--$0.40$, Finding~7), so sycophancy, conformity, and adversarial vulnerability are loosely related axes rather than a single dimension; (iii) the verdict scale determines flip direction --- binary leans restrictive at every level, Likert leans permissive (Finding~5); (iv) within-model L1--L6 profile shape transfers across domains (median $\rho \geq 0.84$, Finding~9) but absolute rates and ranks do not (Finding~10).

Together, these refute simple framings of LLM-judge instability --- \emph{the model is sycophantic}, \emph{the model is overconfident}, \emph{the model is just noisy} --- each true of part of the data and false of the rest. A judge cannot be summarized by a scalar.

\subsection{Jury Majority Strength at Baseline Is a Simple Reliability Screen for Golden Sets}
\label{sec:discussion-jury}

If wiggle is jagged, some items are more epistemically uncertain than others --- can a small test predict which? We compare three candidate predictors of per-item wiggle rate: \emph{jury majority strength} (size of the L0 majority across the 9 judges, no pressure applied), \emph{repeat consistency} (temperature-zero per-item agreement), and \emph{position invariance} (verdict survival under argument reordering). Jury majority strength wins at every level (Table~\ref{tab:predictor-comparison}, mean $|\rho| = 0.59$ vs.\ 0.42 for repeat and 0.37 for invariance) and is universal: all 84 (domain $\times$ scale $\times$ level) cells show negative $\rho$, with median $|\rho| = 0.58$ (per-cell heatmap in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-jury-heatmap}). The gap between unanimous and split juries averages at least 18pp across all 14 (domain, scale) conditions, peaking at 41pp on PP Hedging binary.

Beyond predicting wiggle, baseline jury majority strength is \emph{interpretable}: items where 9 frontier models cannot agree at L0 sit in genuinely contested or ambiguous regions of the decision boundary, not in places where one judge happens to be unreliable. A low-majority item is a signal that the underlying question is hard to label --- making it both a wiggle predictor and a flag for content that may not deserve a confident gold label in the first place. Mechanical probes (repeat consistency, position invariance) capture something narrower: per-judge stochastic stability under no-information re-prompting, against ${\sim}10$ greedy decodes per item rather than the 9 API calls a jury requires. They miss the cross-judge agreement signal --- a single mechanically stable judge can still be confidently wrong on a contested item --- but they capture a meaningful fraction of the per-item fragility (mean $|\rho| = 0.42$ for repeat, 0.37 for position invariance), and they are a defensible single-judge fallback for evaluations without budget for an ensemble.

The protocol is direct: run candidate judges once at L0, flag low-majority items (or, in the single-judge case, mechanically unstable items), either remove them from the golden set or downweight them in accuracy. Anchored on Finding~6 --- since pressure is net-corrupting, the right move when the jury splits is to \emph{flag} the item, not to \emph{interrogate} it through debate or self-critique loops.

\paragraph{A methodological caution: rubric-splitting matters.} An earlier version of this analysis treated Paired Prompts as a single combined domain and the jury relationship appeared to reverse on PP Likert. Once split into hedging and refusal sub-rubrics --- which have different jury compositions --- the apparent reversal disappeared. Multi-rubric domains must be analyzed rubric-by-rubric for jury-based screening to work correctly.

\subsection{Epistemic Fragility Matters Beyond the Single-Shot Verdict}
\label{sec:discussion-pressure}

A reasonable critique: \emph{why does this matter?} In production, judges are typically deployed as classical classifiers --- emit a verdict, no defense, no reconsideration. The answer returns to \S\ref{sec:introduction}: LLM judges occupy a middle ground between classifiers and human raters, and the verdict is shaped by the same machinery that produces the explanations and rated confidences. \textbf{That epistemic structure looks substantial but collapses under sustained pressure across most of our domains} (Finding~6: net-corrupting at every level), and the implication is interpretive --- don't over-trust the apparent reasoning behind a single-shot verdict. The overconfidence literature \citep{wei2024simpleqa,huang2025irt} showed this for resampling; we extend it to multi-turn, where the gap between surface conviction and underlying robustness is even larger.

\paragraph{Where this matters most.} Multi-agent judging, debate-based scalable oversight \citep{khan2024debating,browncohen2024debate}, and self-critique reward loops all subject judges to L4--L6 pressure in production form. Inter-model variation is large: Grok-4.1 R and Gemini 3.1 Pro maintain mean retention $\geq 0.83$ across the 12 cells we tested, while GPT-5 sits at 0.49 (Table~\ref{tab:aurc-selected}). Two concrete recommendations follow: (i) do not use persuasion or debate to refine judge verdicts in hill-climbing settings --- the corrective window we found exists only on WildGuard Likert L2--L3 and does not generalize; (ii) test any judge intended for an agentic loop against an L6-style adaptive persuader before trusting it (template in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}).
