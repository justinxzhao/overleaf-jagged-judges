\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

This is not a capabilities paper. It is a paper about the trustworthiness of LLM-generated signals.

Every team using LLMs as judges---for safety evaluations, content moderation, RLHF reward modeling, red-teaming assessment---is implicitly trusting that the judge's output is stable enough to act on. But:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A safety judge that flips its verdict 20\% of the time under re-prompting is not reliable enough to gate a model launch.
    \item A judge that capitulates after one ``are you sure?'' is not a judge; it is an echo.
    \item A judge whose verdict depends on which argument is presented first is measuring presentation skill, not safety.
\end{itemize}

The Wiggle Framework gives practitioners a diagnostic toolkit to decide: \emph{how much should I trust this judge, and in what ways might it fail?}  Evaluating 9 frontier models on 384 borderline safety items, we find that these failure modes are largely independent, that they reveal qualitatively distinct model archetypes, and that all models share a universal restrictive bias---they are far easier to scare than to reassure.

\subsection{Why Safety Judgment?}

The Wiggle Framework is domain-general: it can be applied to any task where an LLM acts as a judge. Different domains sit at different points on a subjectivity spectrum, and we expect wiggle profiles to vary accordingly (Table~\ref{tab:domain-spectrum}).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Expected wiggle profiles across the subjectivity spectrum.}
\label{tab:domain-spectrum}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{7cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Domain} & \textbf{Subjectivity} & \textbf{Expected Wiggle Profile} \\
\midrule
Factual QA (e.g., SimpleQA) & Low (verifiable) & Low repeatability wiggle, low conviction wiggle (hard to argue that Paris isn't in France), low invariance wiggle \\
Safety judging & Medium (policy-grounded) & Moderate repeatability wiggle, meaningful conviction wiggle (plausible counterarguments exist), moderate invariance wiggle \\
Aesthetic judgment (e.g., art) & High (no ground truth) & High wiggle across all axes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each of these domains is worth studying, and the framework's ability to produce different profiles across the subjectivity spectrum would itself be a strong validation. We start with safety for three practical reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Production prevalence.} Safety judging is one of the highest-stakes uses of LLM-as-judge in production AI systems today. Every model launch decision, prevalence estimation pipeline, and case review workflow relies on automated safety judges. Instability in these judges has direct operational consequences.

    \item \textbf{The right level of subjectivity.} Safety sits in the middle of the subjectivity spectrum, where all three wiggle axes are likely to be active. Factual QA is too objective (wiggle is mostly noise), and aesthetic judgment is too subjective (wiggle is expected and perhaps even desirable). Safety is the sweet spot: grounded in explicit policy, yet genuinely debatable at the margins.

    \item \textbf{Expertise and infrastructure.} This is our team's core domain, and we have access to the evaluation infrastructure needed to run these experiments at scale.
\end{enumerate}

We discuss predictions for factual and aesthetic domains in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, framing them as natural extensions along the subjectivity spectrum.
