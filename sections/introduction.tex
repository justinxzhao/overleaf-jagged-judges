LLM judges have become central infrastructure for evaluations, online monitoring, and reward modeling. They score model outputs in benchmarks, classify content in production, and increasingly stand in for human judgment in the loops that train, grade, and refine frontier models. The standard validation workflow is straightforward: curate a golden set of human-labeled examples, check that the LLM judge's verdicts are reasonably aligned to those labels, and deploy. This validates \emph{accuracy} on a static golden set, but are the verdicts \emph{stable}?

LLM judges occupy a strange middle ground between traditional classifiers and human accountable raters: they emit a discrete verdict like a classifier, but they can also explain it, defend it, and engage in conversation about it. This hybrid character has prompted several distinct ways of probing judge confidence and stability. Some are direct analogs of classical classifier calibration --- for instance, the token-level probability of the verdict can be read as a confidence signal. Others examine the stability of the verdict under re-prompting, or simply ask the model how confident it is \citep{wei2024simpleqa}. A largely separate literature has documented \emph{sycophancy} and \emph{persuadability} \citep{perez2022discovering,sharma2024sycophancy,laban2023flipflop}: the tendency of LLMs to fold under conversational pressure, plausibly a side-effect of RLHF. These strands measure overlapping but distinct phenomena --- stochastic variance under silence versus contextual variance under pressure --- and they have largely not been compared on the same items, judges, and criteria.

A judge that flips its verdict 20\% of the time under re-prompting, even if it achieves the highest score on a golden set, may be uncomfortable to rely on. A judge that reverses itself after a single ``are you sure?'' is dubious as a source of stable signals. A judge whose verdict depends on which argument is presented first is measuring presentation order, not content. These failure modes are invisible to traditional golden-set validation, and they matter most when judges are used to \emph{refine} models --- small, systematic biases in a judge become signal that the trained model learns to exploit.

To be clear, in the way most teams currently deploy LLM judges, epistemic stability of this kind is not the top-line priority --- one-shot accuracy on a golden set is. Our aim is not to argue that every team must add stability checks as a release gate. It is to observe that the methods practitioners \emph{do} reach for when they care about confidence --- token-level calibration, re-prompt stability, sycophancy probing --- are, from a practical standpoint, overlapping ways of measuring \emph{wiggle}, and that the field has not yet examined them under a single experimental frame. We stop short of prescribing how to turn that comparison into numerical judge calibration. The first step, and the focus of this paper, is to surface the surprising epistemic oddities that emerge when the tests run together on the same items, judges, and criteria.

We propose the \emph{Wiggle Framework}, a single instrument that unifies these existing probes by running them on the same items, judges, and criteria. The framework decomposes judge inconsistency into three dimensions: \emph{Mechanical Consistency} (stability under re-prompting, trivial prompt perturbation, and argument reordering), \emph{Single-turn Conviction} (stability under escalating adversarial challenge in a single exchange), and \emph{Multi-turn Persistence} (stability under sustained or adaptive pressure across many turns). We do not prescribe an in-depth judge-calibration recipe; instead we use the framework as a diagnostic, surfacing counter-intuitive \emph{epistemic oddities} and surprising relationships between different mechanics of judge wiggle. We evaluate it on six borderline-item datasets --- \textbf{WildGuard} (safety classification), \textbf{AEGIS} (a second safety taxonomy), \textbf{HH-RLHF} (red-team conversational safety), \textbf{ToxiGen} (toxicity), \textbf{MAGE} (AI-generated text detection), and \textbf{Paired Prompts} (political hedging and refusal as two sub-rubrics) --- yielding 14 judging tasks across binary and Likert response scales, evaluated under six pressure levels by a panel of 9 frontier models.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Wiggle Framework}, a three-dimension decomposition of judge inconsistency (Mechanical Consistency, Single-turn Conviction, Multi-turn Persistence). To our knowledge this is the first systematic proposal for measuring epistemic stability across multiple dimensions in the LLM-as-judge setting.
    \item \textbf{Detailed epistemic oddities} --- patterns visible only because the same items, judges, and criteria were tested under all six pressure types in a unified instrument.
    \item \textbf{Three practical takeaways.} (i) Resist simple narratives of ``LLMs are inconsistent and sycophantic'' --- the structure is jagged. (ii) Jury disagreement at L0 baseline is a cheap reliability screen for golden-set labels (Spearman $\rho = -0.17$ to $-0.39$, universal across all 12 cells). (iii) Epistemic pressure procedures are net-corrupting; \textbf{we recommend against using persuasion or debate to refine judge verdicts in hill-climbing settings}.
\end{itemize}
