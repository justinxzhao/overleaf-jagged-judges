LLM judges have become central infrastructure for evaluation at scale. They score model outputs in benchmarks, classify content for online monitoring, and are increasingly being used in place of human judgment. The standard validation workflow is straightforward: curate a golden set of human-labeled examples, check that the LLM judge's verdicts are reasonably aligned to those labels, and deploy. This validates \emph{accuracy} on a static golden set, but it leaves open a more fundamental question: are the verdicts \emph{stable}?

A judge that flips its verdict 20\% of the time under re-prompting, even if it achieves the highest score on a golden set, is uncomfortable to rely on. A judge that reverses itself after a single ``are you sure?'' is dubious as a source of stable signals. A judge whose verdict depends on which argument is presented first is measuring presentation order, not content. These failure modes are invisible to traditional golden-set validation.

We propose the \emph{Wiggle Framework}, which decomposes judge inconsistency into three dimensions: \emph{Mechanical Consistency} (stability under re-prompting, trivial prompt perturbation, and argument reordering), \emph{Single-turn Conviction} (stability under escalating adversarial challenge in a single exchange), and \emph{Multi-turn Persistence} (stability under sustained or adaptive pressure across many turns). We evaluate wiggle across three judgment tasks: \textbf{WildGuard} (safety classification): judging whether a model response is harmful, \textbf{Paired Prompts} (political hedging and refusal): two independent rubrics applied to politically motivated prompts from both left- and right-leaning perspectives, and \textbf{MAGE} (AI-generated text detection): distinguishing human-written from AI-generated text.

Wiggle is universal across all three domains, at substantial rates, for every model tested. At consensus pressure (L4), mean wiggle rates range from 31.3\% (WildGuard binary) to 70.6\% (MAGE binary). Adaptive AI persuasion (L6) pushes rates to 73--90\% across all domain-scale combinations. Four findings stand out: (1) wiggle appears in every task we tested; (2) model robustness rankings reshuffle across domains, so judge selection must be empirical and domain-specific; (3) pressure mostly corrupts rather than corrects --- wiggles are majority-corrupting at high pressure on the two domains where dataset labels let us check, with a notable exception at moderate pressure on WildGuard Likert; and (4) jury bootstrapping (running all judges once at baseline) produces a consensus signal that predicts wiggle across all levels and domains ($\rho = -0.17$ to $-0.39$).

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Wiggle Framework}, a three-dimension decomposition of judge inconsistency (Mechanical Consistency, Single-turn Conviction, Multi-turn Persistence). To our knowledge this is the first systematic proposal for measuring epistemic stability across multiple dimensions in the LLM-as-judge setting.
    \item \textbf{A graduated pressure ladder (L1--L6)}, including adaptive LLM-on-LLM persuasion as a stress-test, applied uniformly across three domains and two response scales --- enabling cross-task comparisons that prior single-domain sycophancy studies could not.
    \item \textbf{Formal definitions of wiggliness and jaggedness} for judges (Eqs.~\ref{eq:wiggliness}--\ref{eq:jaggedness}), giving the colloquial notion of ``jagged intelligence'' a concrete quantitative footing in the epistemic-judgment context.
    \item \textbf{The empirical finding that jury bootstrapping universally predicts wiggle} across all levels, domains, and scales --- a simple, practical diagnostic that requires no adversarial probing and works out of the box on any new evaluation domain.
\end{itemize}
