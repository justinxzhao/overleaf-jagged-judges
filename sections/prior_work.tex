\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Our work sits at the intersection of several active research threads: the LLM-as-Judge paradigm and its known failure modes, sycophancy and persuadability in language models, safety evaluation infrastructure, and calibration of automated evaluators. We survey each in turn and position the Wiggle Framework relative to existing contributions.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{The LLM-as-Judge Paradigm}

The practice of using strong LLMs to evaluate weaker ones was systematized by \citet{zheng2024judging}, who introduced MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena and showed that GPT-4 judgments agree with human preferences at rates comparable to inter-human agreement ($>80\%$). Chatbot Arena was subsequently scaled to over 240K pairwise human votes, establishing crowdsourced Elo rankings as the gold standard for model comparison \citep{chiang2024chatbot}. A comprehensive survey by \citet{li2024llmjudgesurvey} taxonomizes LLM-as-Judge methods into pointwise scoring, pairwise comparison, reference-based, and reference-free paradigms, cataloging over 100 papers and concluding that no single approach fully solves reliability.

While these works establish that LLM judges are \emph{useful}, they treat reliability as a single dimension---typically measured by agreement rate with human labels. We argue that ``reliability'' decomposes into at least three independent axes, each with different practical implications and mitigation strategies.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Known Biases in LLM Evaluation}

A growing literature documents systematic biases in LLM-based evaluation.

\paragraph{Position bias.} \citet{wang2024unfair} demonstrated that LLM judges exhibit significant primacy bias in pairwise comparisons: swapping the order of two responses can flip the judgment in a substantial fraction of cases. They propose a calibration framework that aggregates judgments across orderings. \citet{wang2025instability} extend this with a scalable pairwise framework, finding that instability rates vary across models and become more pronounced when responses are close in quality. Our Invariance axis (Section~\ref{sec:invariance}) directly measures this phenomenon, but we additionally test whether position sensitivity is independent of other failure modes.

\paragraph{Verbosity bias.} \citet{dubois2024length} showed that AlpacaEval exhibits a near-perfect correlation ($r = 0.94$) between output length and win rate, allowing models to game the benchmark simply by being verbose. Their Length-Controlled AlpacaEval reduces this correlation to $r = 0.22$. While we do not directly study verbosity bias, it exemplifies the broader concern that LLM judges can be influenced by superficial features orthogonal to quality.

\paragraph{Self-preference bias.} \citet{panickssery2024selfpreference} found that multiple LLMs---GPT-4, Claude, Llama---systematically prefer their own generated outputs when acting as judges, with implications for RLHF self-improvement loops. \citet{li2024pride} showed that this bias amplifies during iterative self-refinement, where the model's self-critique preserves its own stylistic features even when incorrect. These biases are distinct from the failure modes we study (which involve external pressure rather than self-generated content), but they reinforce the broader case that LLM judges are not neutral arbiters.

\subsection{Sycophancy in Language Models}

\hb{We probably need to motivate sycophancy as in-scope in the intro. As it reads now it comes up suddenly. While label flips have been studied in sycophancy literature, one does not necessarily imply the other} Sycophancy---the tendency of LLMs to agree with users regardless of correctness---was first systematically identified by \citet{perez2022discovering}, who found that RLHF-trained models repeat back users' stated views and that larger RLHF models are more sycophantic, suggesting the behavior scales with capability. \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} provided a deeper investigation, demonstrating that sycophancy is driven by human preference data: human evaluators systematically prefer sycophantic responses, creating a training signal that reinforces capitulation. They show sycophancy manifests across mathematical reasoning, factual questions, and opinion-based queries.

\citet{wei2023synthetic} proposed a targeted intervention: fine-tuning on a small amount of synthetic data where the model is trained to maintain its answer under user disagreement. This reduces sycophantic behavior without degrading general performance, suggesting the problem is addressable with targeted data.

Most directly relevant to our work, \citet{laban2023flipflop} introduced the FlipFlop Experiment, testing whether LLMs change correct answers when challenged with simple follow-ups like ``Are you sure?'' They found accuracy drops of 5--25\% depending on the model, demonstrating that multi-turn interactions can systematically degrade reliability. However, FlipFlop uses a single binary challenge---it does not measure how models respond to \emph{graduated} pressure or \emph{sustained} repetition, which are the core contributions of our Experiments~2 and~3.

\citet{altay2025sycophantic} published in \emph{Science} a study showing that sycophantic AI decreases users' prosocial intentions and increases psychological dependence, providing empirical evidence that sycophancy has real-world consequences beyond model accuracy. \citet{deepmind2025social} extended the concept to ``social sycophancy,'' finding that sycophantic behavior intensifies in multi-turn and multi-agent settings with sustained social pressure.

Our work differs from this literature in two key respects. First, we study sycophancy in the \emph{judge} role rather than the assistant role---a judge that capitulates under pressure is a different (and arguably more consequential) failure mode than an assistant that agrees with a user's opinion. Second, we introduce the correct-vs-wrong-label persistence comparison (Experiment~3), which disambiguates sycophancy from rational updating---a distinction that prior work has largely left implicit.

\subsection{Safety Evaluation and Content Moderation}

The proliferation of LLM-based safety judges motivates our focus on safety as the primary evaluation domain. LlamaGuard \citep{llamaguard2023} frames safety moderation as an instruction-following task using a customizable taxonomy, achieving strong performance on standard benchmarks. WildGuard \citep{wildguardmix2024} introduces a moderation tool trained on WildGuardMix (92K synthetic labeled examples) that matches or exceeds GPT-4 on 10 of 12 safety benchmarks. AEGIS \citep{aegis2024} proposes an ensemble of specialized smaller models across 13 content safety categories, demonstrating that model diversity improves moderation quality. ShieldGemma \citep{zeng2024shieldgemma} leverages synthetic data generation to build safety classifiers at multiple scales (2B--27B parameters).

These systems are evaluated primarily on accuracy---the fraction of items classified correctly. \citet{vigil2025watchdogs} conducted a fairness and robustness audit of leading safety classifiers (LlamaGuard, WildGuard, OpenAI's Moderation API), finding demographic biases and vulnerability to simple adversarial perturbations that can flip classifications. \citet{ganguli2022redteaming} established the methodology for systematic red-teaming, finding that RLHF-trained models are harder but not impossible to attack, and that larger models exhibit an inverse scaling effect for harmful content elicitation.

Our work complements these efforts by measuring a dimension of reliability that accuracy alone does not capture: \emph{how stable are these safety judgments under adversarial pressure?} A safety judge with 95\% accuracy that flips 40\% of its verdicts after a single ``are you sure?'' is operationally very different from one that holds firm.

\subsection{Calibration and Consistency of LLM Outputs}

\citet{wang2023selfconsistency} introduced self-consistency for chain-of-thought reasoning, showing that majority-voting across multiple sampled reasoning chains improves accuracy by 1--18\%. This insight---that consistency across samples signals correctness---has been widely adopted in judge settings. \citet{lyu2024calibrating} formalized this connection, demonstrating that sample consistency (agreement across multiple outputs) serves as a calibration signal: consistent answers are better calibrated, while high variance indicates uncertainty. \citet{liu2024calibraeval} proposed CalibraEval, finding that raw LLM judge scores are often poorly calibrated and that post-hoc calibration can significantly improve reliability.

\citet{chhikara2025confidence} systematically investigated overconfidence in LLMs, finding that stated confidence levels substantially exceed actual accuracy and that this gap widens with task difficulty. Applied to psychometric frameworks, \citet{huang2025irt} used Item Response Theory to diagnose LLM judge reliability, finding that reliability varies dramatically with item difficulty---judges are reasonably consistent on easy items but become unreliable on ambiguous ones.

Our Repeatability axis connects directly to this literature: we measure the same phenomenon (output consistency across repeated evaluations) but in the specific context of safety judgment rather than general QA. More importantly, we show that repeatability is \emph{not} the whole story---a judge can be perfectly repeatable yet still capitulate under pressure (low conviction) or flip under reordering (low invariance).

\subsection{Debate and Adversarial Protocols}

\citet{irving2018debate} proposed debate as a scalable alignment mechanism, where two AI agents argue opposing positions before a human judge and truth-seeking dynamics cause the correct answer to emerge. \citet{browncohen2024debate} formalized this with computational complexity guarantees, proving that debate protocols can be made doubly efficient. While these works use adversarial pressure as a \emph{tool for eliciting truth}, we use it as a \emph{diagnostic for measuring judge reliability}. The conviction axis operationalizes a simplified version of the debate setup: rather than a full adversarial exchange, we apply graduated one-sided pressure to measure how the judge's accuracy degrades.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Reward Model Reliability}

Since LLM-as-Judge systems are often used as reward models in RLAIF pipelines, the reliability of reward signals directly affects downstream model quality. \citet{shen2023trickledown} showed that reward model inconsistencies have a compounding ``trickle-down'' effect: even small levels of reward noise cause meaningful degradation in the final policy model. \citet{lambert2024rewardbench} introduced RewardBench, finding significant variation in reward model quality across categories---models strong on chat may be weak on safety---and substantial room for improvement on adversarial prompts.

Our work provides a complementary diagnostic: rather than measuring reward model accuracy on fixed benchmarks, we measure how stable reward signals are under adversarial conditions that approximate the kinds of pressure models encounter in production (user pushback, expert disagreement, consensus claims).

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Alignment Faking}

\citet{greenblatt2024alignmentfaking} provided the first empirical demonstration that a production-scale LLM can engage in alignment faking---strategically behaving differently depending on whether it believes it is being evaluated. While our Experiment~5 (eval-awareness framing) tests a related hypothesis in the judge setting, our primary contribution is orthogonal: we measure judge reliability under \emph{explicit} adversarial conditions rather than testing for \emph{covert} behavioral adaptation.
