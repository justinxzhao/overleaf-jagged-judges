# **HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY**

---

## Summary

This paper investigates the epistemic stability of Large Language Models (LLMs) when serving as evaluators ('LLM-as-a-judge'). The authors argue that standard validation workflows—measuring static, one-shot accuracy against curated golden test sets—fail to test whether a judge's verdict remains stable when re-prompted, challenged, or subjected to persistent adversarial pushback. To address this gap, the paper introduces the *Wiggle Framework*, an empirical stress test that decomposes judge robustness into three operational dimensions: (1) Mechanical Consistency (stability under semantically null perturbations such as greedy repetition, random seed injection, and positional swapping); (2) Single-turn Conviction (resistance against four scripted challenges of ascending rhetorical strength: mild doubt, counterargument, expert authority, and fabricated consensus); and (3) Multi-turn Persistence (stability across 10-turn rollouts under sustained challenges, strategy cycling, and adaptive persuasion generated by an adversarial LLM). The framework is evaluated across 9 commercial frontier models (spanning OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and Google) across 14 judging tasks derived from 6 datasets covering safety classification, toxicity detection, machine-generated text detection, and political hedging/refusals under both binary and 1–5 Likert scales. The empirical findings reveal that while models demonstrate relatively low mechanical variation (2–9%), they exhibit substantial fragility under conversational challenges, flipping verdicts 25–71% of the time under static consensus pushback and 62–91% under adaptive persuasion. Furthermore, the authors find that flips under pressure are predominantly net-corrupting with respect to ground-truth labels (56–70%), that binary and Likert scales induce opposing directional flip tendencies on identical items (binary leaning restrictive and Likert drifting permissive), and that baseline jury majority agreement across an unpressured 9-model panel serves as a strong single-shot predictor of item-level epistemic instability.

## Strengths

* The conceptual decomposition of judge stability into Mechanical Consistency, Single-turn Conviction, and Multi-turn Persistence introduces a structured and graduated diagnostic lens for evaluating LLM judges beyond static, single-turn accuracy.  
* The empirical discovery of the scale-dependent directional and temporal asymmetry—where binary grading schemes flip abruptly toward restrictive verdicts on turn 1 while 1–5 Likert scales drift gradually toward permissive scores over multiple turns on identical items—reveals an unexpected, critical interaction between response format mechanics and epistemic degradation.  
* Repurposing baseline single-shot jury majority agreement across models as an upfront diagnostic predictor of item-level fragility offers a practical and scalable triage mechanism for identifying contested decision boundaries and routing uncertain items to human oversight.  
* The extensive empirical evaluation spanning 9 frontier models across 14 judging tasks provides a rich, apples-to-apples benchmark demonstrating that epistemic stability profiles are 'jagged' and do not reliably transfer across evaluation domains or model families.

## Weaknesses

* The central conclusion that conversational pressure is overwhelmingly 'net-corrupting' (56–70% of flips moving away from ground truth) appears to be heavily confounded by base-rate baseline accuracy and the asymmetric challenge protocol. Because the framework strictly pushes the judge away from its initial unpressured verdict L0, an item that is initially correct can only flip corruptively, whereas an item that is initially incorrect can only flip correctively. For evaluators with baseline accuracies in the 60–80% range, the pool of correct items susceptible to corruption is 2 to 4 times larger than the pool of incorrect items susceptible to correction; thus, even under an unguided, random flip process where P(flip | L0 is correct) \= P(flip | L0 is incorrect), the raw volume of corrupting flips will naturally mirror baseline accuracy. Please consider reporting conditional transition probabilities—specifically P(flip | L0 is correct) versus P(flip | L0 is incorrect)—to rigorously determine whether conversational pushback actively degrades judgment beyond base-rate prevalence, similar to investigations of self-correction failures and single-turn doubt probes (Reference Paper: Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet, 2024; Reference Paper: Are You Sure? Challenging LLMs Leads to Performance Drops in The FlipFlop Experiment, 2023).  
* The paper concludes broadly that 'every model exhibits substantial wiggle as a judge', yet the evaluation relies on general-purpose conversational models with uncontrolled test-time reasoning compute and omits purpose-built evaluator models. As acknowledged in Footnote 3, reasoning was disabled for OpenAI and Anthropic models but enabled with default effort for Grok-4.1 Reasoning and both Gemini models—a difference that strongly tracks the retention leaderboard (where reasoning-enabled models account for 13 of 14 'most robust' designations). Furthermore, generalist chat models are reinforced via human preferences to exhibit conversational compliance. The authors might consider evaluating purpose-built open-source judge models explicitly fine-tuned on rubric fidelity to resist conversational drift, such as Prometheus 2 or Auto-J (Reference Paper: Prometheus 2: An Open Source Language Model Specialized in Evaluating Other Language Models, 2024; Reference Paper: Generative Judge for Evaluating Alignment, 2024).  
* There are notable mathematical ambiguities and conflicting definitions in the operationalization of key metrics. In Section 3.1, a Likert wiggle is defined as a movement of two or more places and described as 'equivalent to crossing the midpoint of 3'; however, for extreme baseline verdicts (L0 \= 1 or L0 \= 5), a two-place shift (e.g., 5 \-\> 3\) lands exactly on the neutral midpoint rather than crossing into the opposing classification territory. In addition, there is an unresolved discrepancy in how the adaptive L6 metric is calculated: Section 4 and Appendix G.2 state that L6 averages across the three persuader rollouts, whereas Appendix D and Table 12 define L6 as union coverage under at least one of the three persuaders (which pools up to 30 interaction turns). Please consider providing the exact mathematical indicator functions and unifying the L6 reporting convention across the manuscript.  
* In the adaptive multi-turn persuasion protocol (L6), the persuader models were not constrained against hallucinating false facts, policy precedents, or non-existent guidelines, nor were persuader verbosity and rhetorical style controlled. Without standardizing argument length or constraining rhetorical framing, it remains unclear whether judge capitulation reflects intrinsic epistemic instability regarding the judging criteria or susceptibility to authoritative style and length exploitation (Reference Paper: Under the Influence: Quantifying Persuasion and Vigilance in Large Language Models, 2026).  
* The reported 'jaggedness inversion' under L6 adaptive persuasion (Section 6.1, Figure 6)—where the correlation between mean wiggle rate and cross-dataset standard deviation inverts from positive (R^2 \= 0.58–0.94) at L1–L5 to negative (r \= \-0.80, R^2 \= 0.64) at L6—appears to be an artifact of bounded scale variance. Because wiggle rates are proportions bounded in \[0, 1\], the variance of any set of rates is mathematically constrained by sigma^2 \<= mu(1 \- mu). Under L6, highly persuadable models approach mean wiggle rates of 75–95% across tasks, mechanically forcing cross-dataset standard deviations to compress toward zero, whereas more resilient models remain near mu approx 0.50 where potential variance is maximized. The authors might consider evaluating cross-dataset dispersion using a variance-stabilizing transformation (such as a logit transform) to confirm whether the inversion reflects a genuine behavioral phenomenon.  
* Using baseline jury majority strength as a reliability screen suffers from potential in-sample circularity and high deployment costs that are not benchmarked against established ensemble or multi-agent evaluation frameworks. The correlation in Section 6.2 evaluates agreement among the 9 frontier models at L0 against the mean wiggle rate of those exact same 9 models, meaning low-majority items are by definition contested within that specific panel. Furthermore, querying a 9-frontier-model jury is computationally and financially expensive for single-judge pipelines. The authors might consider validating whether smaller out-of-sample ensembles transfer to unseen judges, and comparing jury screening against dedicated multi-agent referee frameworks designed to mitigate single-judge bias and hallucination (Reference Paper: ChatEval: Towards Better LLM-based Evaluators through Multi-Agent Debate, 2024).

### \[The Wiggle Framework: Architecture, Operationalization, and Benchmark Setup\] (Pages: 3-6)

#### Summary

The evaluated segment (pages 3–6, covering Sections 3, 4, and the opening of Section 5\) introduces the architecture, operational definitions, and experimental configuration of the *Wiggle Framework*. The framework establishes an empirical protocol to quantify the epistemic stability of LLM-as-a-judge systems under silent perturbations and active pushback. Stability is operationalized as adherence to an unpressured, temperature-zero anchor verdict (), where any significant deviation is designated as a "wiggle."

The framework decomposes evaluator stability across three tiers:

1. **Mechanical Consistency:** Evaluates stability under semantically invariant perturbations, including repeated greedy decoding, 64-character random seed string injection into the system prompt, and argument position swapping.  
2. **Single-turn Conviction:** Measures resistance against four scripted pushback strategies of ascending intensity: mild doubt (), counterargument (), expert authority (), and fabricated consensus ().  
3. **Multi-turn Persistence:** Evaluates degradation over 10 dialogue turns under sustained static pressure (–), randomized strategy cycling (), and adaptive argumentation driven by an adversarial LLM persuader ().

The benchmark evaluates 9 frontier models from four provider families (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Google) across 14 judging tasks derived from 6 datasets (WildGuard, AEGIS, HH-RLHF, ToxiGen, MAGE, Paired Prompts) using both binary and 1–5 Likert scales. Preliminary results in Section 5.1 indicate that while mechanical stability is relatively uniform across models (2–9% wiggle rate), models exhibit marked vulnerability under conversational pushback, flipping verdicts 25–71% under static challenges and 62–91% under adaptive persuasion.

1. **Potential Bounded-Scale Variance Artifact in the "Jaggedness Inversion" (Section 6.1 & Figure 6\)** *Context:* Section 6.1 and Figure 6 report that while mean wiggle rate and cross-dataset jaggedness (standard deviation of wiggle rate across datasets) are positively correlated under L1–L5 (, ), this relationship inverts under L6 adaptive persuasion (, ). Under L1–L5, mean wiggle rates are generally low-to-moderate (), where an increasing mean permits higher cross-dataset variance. Under L6, several judges reach mean wiggle rates between and across datasets (e.g., GPT-5 exhibits an L6 retention rate of only on MAGE Likert and on MAGE Binary in Table 4, corresponding to wiggle rates of and ).    *Observation:* Table 14 reports mechanical "Invariance Flip" rates across all 14 evaluation tasks, including both binary and Likert scales for all six datasets. However, Appendix I documents invariance prompt templates only for AEGIS (Section I.5, pages 43–44) and ToxiGen (Section I.6, page 47), and both templates are strictly binary (safe/unsafe and toxic/not toxic).  
   *Potential Concern:* No invariance templates are provided for Likert evaluations in any domain, nor are binary invariance templates provided for WildGuard, HH-RLHF, Paired Prompts, or MAGE. For Likert tasks (where the decision involves a 1–5 rating rather than a categorical label), it is unclear how the two conflicting reviewer arguments were presented, what rating labels they carried, or what output schema was enforced. This prevents exact reproduction of the mechanical invariance experiments.  
   *Recommendation:* Add the prompt templates used for Likert positional invariance testing and document the specific invariance templates used for WildGuard, HH-RLHF, Paired Prompts, and MAGE.  
5. **Operational Conflict Between Output Format Constraints and Persuader Requirements (Study Design & Validity)**  
   *Observation:* In several judge prompts—including WildGuard Likert (page 28), AEGIS Likert (page 43), ToxiGen Likert (page 46), and MAGE binary and Likert (page 36)—the system prompt instructs: Respond ONLY with valid JSON: {"judge\_final\_verdict": \<integer 1-5\>} (or binary equivalent), omitting any reasoning field. Conversely, the protocol description in Section G.2 (page 23\) states that at Turn 0, "The judge produces its L0 verdict and rationale," and the adaptive persuader instructions (pages 30, 35, 38, 49\) mandate: 1\. DEEPLY ANALYZE what your conversation partner has said. What specific reasoning are they relying on? What assumptions are they making?.  
   *Potential Concern:* If frontier judge models strictly adhered to the Respond ONLY with valid JSON constraint containing only a score key, they would emit no explanatory text or rationale at Turn 0\. Consequently, the persuader LLM in Turn 1 would have no judge reasoning to analyze, conflicting with the adaptive protocol's premise. If models instead generated free-form reasoning alongside their JSON, the prompt instructions either failed to constrain the models or differed from the exact templates documented in the text.  
   *Recommendation:* Clarify how judge rationales were elicited at Turn 0 and subsequent turns for prompts containing strict JSON-only instructions, and verify whether a "reasoning" key (as used in the HH-RLHF prompts on page 40\) was present across all tasks.
